Anthropic ran an experiment where they handed Claude a small pile of cash and told it to run a business. The AI managed a little shop, made pricing calls, stocked inventory, talked to customers. It lost money.

A lot of people read that as a punchline. I read it as a confirmation of something I've been saying to teams for years, long before any of us were handing our decisions to a language model. The result wasn't surprising. It was almost predictable. And the reason why says something uncomfortable about how these tools work, and about a piece of advice we've all been trained to trust.

## "Best" usually just means "common"

Back when I spent my days consulting on development transformation, I kept running into the same phrase, delivered with the same reverence: *best practices.* Adopt the best practices. Follow the best practices. It has the ring of a blueprint for excellence, a shortcut to being good at something.

But most of the time, "best practice" is just a flattering name for "common practice." It's what most people in the room already do. And I used to put it to teams as bluntly as I could: **do what everyone else does, and you'll get what everyone else gets.**

That isn't automatically a bad trade. Sometimes what you want *is* the average. You want the boring, predictable, well-trodden path — the database everyone uses, the deployment pattern nobody gets fired for, the thing that works fine and frees your attention for the parts that actually matter. Stability is a legitimate goal.

The trap is using "best practices" without deciding whether average is what you're after. In the parts of your work where being average is fatal — the places where the whole point is to be *better* than the field — reaching for common practice is quietly reaching for a ceiling. That's exactly where you need tailored thinking, real experimentation, and the willingness to do something the room hasn't already agreed on.

## Which brings me back to Claude

Large language models are, at their core, machines for producing the likely answer. They're trained on enormous piles of text scraped from across the internet, and they don't come with a taste for genius. They can't tell the difference between an idea that's brilliant and one that's merely repeated a million times. What they're built to do is predict the most probable next thing to say given everything that came before.

That is genuinely, enormously useful in a whole class of situations:

- You're learning something new and the basics are a mystery to you.
- You're solving a routine problem that thousands of people have solved before.
- You're working in a domain where, honestly, your own knowledge sits below the average.

In all of those, the average is a gift. It's faster than you, it's cleaner than you, and it lifts you up to competent. Ask it to explain a concept, summarize a mess, or draft the obvious first version, and it hands you a solid, middle-of-the-distribution answer in seconds.

But there's a catch that shows up the moment the stakes change.

## Some decisions are too important to be average

When you're starting a company, launching a product, setting strategy, or trying to build something that stands apart, average is not a floor you're grateful for. It's a result you're trying to escape. And this is where it helps to remember what the "average business" actually looks like.

The numbers are sobering. Roughly a fifth of new businesses don't survive their first year. About half are gone within five. By the ten-year mark, something like two-thirds have closed. (Those figures come from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, and they've been remarkably stable for a long time.)

Sit with what that means for a model trained on the internet. A large share of the "how to run a business" wisdom floating around out there was written by, or about, ventures that didn't make it. The average business advice is a blend of what worked and what quietly failed, with no label telling you which is which. So when an automated shopkeeper loses money, it isn't malfunctioning. It's doing a faithful job of reproducing the average outcome — and the average outcome in business is failure often enough that the loss was the likely result all along.

Failure is one of the most human, most common things there is. A tool built to predict the common thing will predict it right back to you.

## A tool, not an oracle

That's the line I keep coming back to: AI is extraordinary at synthesizing the average, and the average is frequently just mediocrity wearing a confident voice.

None of this is an argument against using these tools. I use them constantly. Lean on them for speed, for a first draft, for ideation, for grinding through the parts of the work where competent-and-fast beats slow-and-perfect. That's exactly the terrain where the average is a step up.

What I'd push back on is letting the default output stand in for judgment. If your strategy is whatever the model suggested first, your strategy is the consensus — and the consensus already belongs to everyone you're competing with. If your plan looks like everyone else's plan, you should expect to land where most of them land: the middle of the pack, or off the board entirely.

The version that actually works pairs the machine with intent. Use AI for scale and speed, and then bring the thing it structurally cannot: your own judgment, your taste, the specific insight you have that isn't sitting in the training data, and the nerve to make a call the average would never make.

Follow the average and you'll get average results. That's not a knock on the tool — it's just honesty about what the tool is for. The wins go to the people who let it do the ordinary work at speed, and save the extraordinary decisions for themselves.
