I used to be the engineer who'd vanish once the puzzle was solved.

The hard part. The algorithm, the data model, the architectural decision that made everything else click. That's where I lived. I loved sitting with a problem until the shape of the solution emerged. And then, somewhere between "it works on my machine" and "a human can actually use this," my attention would quietly walk out of the room.

The docs didn't get written. The deploy was rough. The error messages were notes-to-self. The thing technically worked, but only I knew how to operate it. I didn't think of this as a failure. I didn't think of it at all. The remaining work never rose to the surface of my attention as something that deserved it. It wasn't a decision to defer it or hand it off; it was a failure to register the work as work in the first place. My focus had moved on the moment the puzzle gave way, and whatever was left lived in a kind of blind spot.

I'm writing this in the present tense for a reason. This is a thing I still wrestle with. My natural inclination has not changed. What's changed is that I've come to see the inclination clearly, and I've come to see what it costs.

## The value system under the behavior

If you'd asked me back then why I lost interest after the hard part, I would have given you something about boredom or context-switching. The truth was deeper: I had a value system that ranked *clever* far above *useful*. Solving the difficult middle felt like the real contribution. Everything after felt like janitorial work: the polish, the packaging, the explaining, the shipping. Necessary, maybe, but not the thing.

A lot of engineers carry this value system. We don't talk about it because it sounds bad when you say it out loud. But it shows up in our calendars, our pull requests, and the projects that never quite get over the line.

## The view from the other side

Hundreds of client engagements taught me what this looks like from the other chair. A client doesn't care that the hard part is solved. They care whether the thing is in their hands, doing the job they paid for. An 80% solution that nobody can use is worth zero. A clever middle wrapped in broken edges helps no one.

I knew this intellectually for years before I felt it. What finally made me feel it was running my own ventures. Suddenly I was the one with money on the line, watching someone else disappear after the hard part. I'd find myself wishing, sometimes desperately, for something rough and shipped instead of something perfect and stuck. Just give me the imperfect thing. I can work with imperfect. I can't work with absent.

That frustration was a mirror. I was watching myself from ten years ago, and finally understanding why it had been so hard to be on the receiving end of me.

## The mode I already had

The strangest part of all of this is that I already knew how to work the other way. I just hadn't noticed.

In ministry work, I'd never struggled with finishing. Not because I'd developed some superior discipline there, but because the framing was completely different. I wasn't optimizing a technical challenge. I was meeting a person, or a group of people, with a real need, and the work was whatever it took to actually meet that need. The "long tail" wasn't a tail at all. It was the point.

Same person. Two completely different operating modes. The difference was simple: in one context, a person was at the center. In the other, a problem was.

## The bridge

This is the connector I missed for far too long, and it's the whole thesis of this post:

**Finishing isn't a discipline problem. It's an empathy problem.**

The reason the last mile feels boring is that we're attached to the problem, not the person. When the person is abstract (just "the user," "the client," "whoever ends up using this"), the polish feels optional. When the person is vivid (someone you know, someone whose trust you carry, someone whose Tuesday gets better or worse based on whether you ship), the polish stops feeling like polish. It starts feeling like the actual work.

Paul puts it tighter than I can: *"Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up."* (1 Corinthians 8:1)

I used to read that as a verse about pride in religious circles and miss how exactly it describes the engineer at work. Knowledge puffs up. It inflates the one who has it. You can sit with a hard problem, find the elegant solution, and feel taller for it without anyone else ever benefiting. Love builds up. It constructs something for someone else. Same effort, completely different orientation, completely different result. One feels good privately. The other actually puts something in another person's hands.

*The Trusted Advisor*, by Maister, Green, and Galford, is a secular book that arrives at the same conclusion from a completely different angle. They draw a sharp line between *understanding* a client's problem cerebrally and *relating* to it as a human being who cares about another human being. I had been doing the first my whole career. The second was the thing that turned my work into something people could actually use. Scripture said it. The consulting literature confirmed it. My client work paid for the lesson several times over before I learned it.

## The reward loop

Once you cross this bridge, something shifts that's hard to undo.

Cleverness is a private satisfaction. You can have it alone, in your editor, at midnight, and it counts. Usefulness is a shared one. It requires another person on the other end, picking up your work and doing something with it. The first feels good. The second feels like *meaning*.

I started chasing the second. Not out of professional duty, but because the first stopped being enough. Watching theoretical changes become real differences in someone's day is a different drug, and once you've had it, the puzzle alone goes flat. The desire to see my work used pulled me across the bridge that discipline alone could never have built.

## The principle isn't ours

Almost everything I've written here is about software because that's the chair I sit in. But the principle isn't ours. It's the same shape wherever there is hard work with a long tail.

A kid finishes the obvious part of cleaning up after dinner. Dishes in the sink, food put away. Then they walk off before the counter is wiped, the trash is taken out, the floor is swept. The "real work" felt done. The next family member into the kitchen has a different opinion.

A neighbor cuts the grass and leaves the clippings on the driveway and the trimmer line uncoiled by the fence. The lawn looks better. The yard isn't actually finished.

A contractor frames an addition beautifully and then drags out the punch list for months: paint touch-ups, a drawer that doesn't close, a switch plate hanging crooked. The hard, structural work is done. The clients still can't quite live in the room.

A family member watches a loved one's kids and handles the part that feels like the job, keeping them safe, keeping them fed, but leaves the lunch dishes, the toys across the floor, the laundry sliding off the chair. The parents come home to a different kind of mess than they left.

In every one of these, the same thing is happening. The person doing the work is finished when *their* part feels done. The person on the receiving end is finished when *they* can pick their life back up and keep going. When those two definitions of "done" diverge, somebody is left holding the gap.

Empathy is what closes that gap. Not more discipline, not better tools, not a longer checklist. A willingness to picture the next person, and to let what they need redefine what "done" means.

## Where I am now

I still feel the old pull. I still want to solve the hard part and walk away. But I notice it now, and I notice the shape of the person I'm building for, and most days the second is louder than the first.

I'm also, I'll admit, less patient with people who work the way I used to work. I find myself wanting to say: just ship the imperfect thing. Get it into someone's hands. The clever middle you're polishing matters far less than you think, and the rough edges you're avoiding matter far more.

If you're an engineer who struggles to finish, and a lot of us do, I'd offer this. The fix is probably not more grit. The fix is probably picturing, vividly and specifically, the person on the other end. Hold them in your mind. Let them be real. Then look at the unfinished thing and ask whether what's left is really beneath you, or whether it's just the part that doesn't feel clever.

Finishing well, it turns out, is what empathy looks like in software. And in the kitchen. And in the yard. And anywhere a person is waiting on the other side of someone else's work.
